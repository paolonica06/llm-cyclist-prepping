## Abstract
This thesis investigated the integration of blood flow restriction (BFR) training with high-intensity interval training (HIIT) to enhance physiological and performance adaptations, particularly for male recreational endurance athletes. While endurance training traditionally emphasizes high volumes of low-intensity exercise, HIIT is crucial for improving parameters such as maximal oxygen uptake (VÌ‡O2max), lactate threshold, exercise economy, and anaerobic capacity. Recent research suggests that pairing BFR with HIIT may amplify acute training stimuli, potentially enhancing chronic aerobic, anaerobic, and muscular performance. However, the current literature reveals unanswered questions about the acute and chronic responses to various BFR + HIIT protocols.

BFR and HIIT methodologies vary widely, with different application strategies and exercise protocols producing a range of physiological responses. To address these complexities, this thesis aimed to: (i) evaluate and analyse existing literature on BFR, HIIT, and their combination; (ii) identify feasible BFR + HIIT protocols and assess their acute physiological effects; and (iii) evaluate the chronic physiological and performance outcomes of BFR-HIIT compared to HIIT alone.

Chapter 2 (Review of Literature) reviewed the energetics of endurance activities like middle-distance running and cycling, key physiological determinants of endurance performance, and the mechanisms of BFR training. The systematic review highlighted evidence on the acute and chronic responses to combining BFR with HIIT. Most studies have focused on maximal sprint-based protocols, which differ significantly from submaximal effort aerobic HIIT in terms of metabolic, neuromuscular, and perceptual responses. This underscores the need to investigate aerobic BFR + HIIT protocols to better understand the mechanisms driving chronic adaptations. Additionally, the variability in BFR application methods adds complexity for practitioners seeking to implement effective training strategies.

Study 1 (Chapter 3) compared acute physiological and perceptual responses between three BFR + interval exercise protocols: high-intensity rest occlusion (HIRO), moderate-high intensity high occlusion (MIHO), moderate-high intensity low occlusion (MILO); and a control high-intensity condition. The findings revealed that applying BFR at 80% limb occlusion pressure (LOP) during recovery intervals (HIRO) increased internal physiological stress without altering exercise intensity or perceptual responses. Meanwhile, applying BFR at 80% LOP during moderate-intensity interval exercise induced metabolic stress comparable to HIIT but with reduced muscle activation and mechanical load. A gradual and progressive approach to exercise intensity and BFR pressure was recommended for safe implementation.

Study 2 (Chapter 4) evaluated the chronic adaptations of BFR + HIIT versus a HIIT-only control in male recreational endurance athletes over a three-week, volume-matched nine-session intervention. The results showed that BFR + HIIT, with BFR applied during rest periods, enhanced aerobic adaptations compared to HIIT under sham hypoxic conditions. However, the addition of BFR did not further improve anaerobic capacity or strength development.

In conclusion, the thesis achieved its objectives through a structured series of studies. Integrating BFR into rest periods during endurance-focused HIIT protocols, particularly short-interval aerobic sessions, enhanced acute training stimuli without affecting perceptual responses and led to greater improvements in aerobic performance over nine sessions compared to HIIT alone. These findings provide valuable insights for optimising training strategies in endurance sports.
