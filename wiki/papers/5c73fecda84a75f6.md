## Abstract
INTRODUCTION:
                    Repeated-sprint training in hypoxia (RSH) is increasingly used by elite athletes to improve repeated-sprint ability and power production[1]. RSH involves performing a series of short “all-out” sprints with brief recovery periods[2] in a hypoxic environment[3]. Despite its widespread use, limited research has examined how athlete phenotype influences session responses. This study investigates how sprint performance in RSH differ across athlete profiles.
                  
                  
                    METHODS:
                    Nineteen Australian track endurance cyclists completed field and laboratory tests to establish power-duration profiles and calculate anaerobic power reserve (APR). Athletes undertook a two-week RSH training block (four sessions), completing two key protocols: RSH-6 (4 × 5×6s) and RSH-10 (3 × 5×10s) in a normobaric hypoxia (FiO₂: 14.5%). APR z-scores were used to classify athletes along an endurance-to-sprint spectrum within each sex. Peak power output (PPO), sprint decrement score and individual mean and standard deviations were assessed relative to their APR z-score for both key protocols.
                  
                  
                    RESULTS:
                    Male hybrid- and sprint-profiled athletes produced higher absolute PPO, but sprint-profiled males achieved this at a lower percentage of APR. In females, absolute PPO was similar across profiles; however, sprint-profiled athletes produced lower PPO relative to APR than endurance- and hybrid-profiled teammates. Sprint-profiled athletes demonstrated greater within-athlete variability, with standard deviations of 118–219W (males) and 69–73W (females), compared to endurance-profiled athletes (22–47W males; 25–28W females). APR z-score showed a small positive correlation with sprint decrement score (males=0.32; females=0.46), indicating greater fatigue in sprint-profiled athletes.
                  
                  
                    CONCLUSION:
                    These findings suggest that phenotype significantly affects sprint execution in RSH. Individualised training prescriptions, such as adjusting sprint volume, recovery and duration based on the athlete profile may enhance RSH effectiveness.
