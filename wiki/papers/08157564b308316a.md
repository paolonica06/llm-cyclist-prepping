## Qualità metodologica
- Tipo di studio: **randomized_crossover_trial**
- Qualità: **high** · Confidenza: **moderate** · Trasferibilità ciclisti competitivi: **moderate**
  - sample: n=6 uomini attivi non-elite: campione minimo per studi con isotopi stabili; assenza di donne; non rappresentativo di ciclisti competitivi allenati
  - control: Tre condizioni (L-CHO, PRO-CHO, H-CHO) ben definite con doppio controllo: iso-CHO e iso-energetico; design rigoroso per isolare l'effetto proteico
  - randomization: Crossover randomizzato: riduce variabilità inter-individuale; periodo di wash-out non descritto esplicitamente nel testo fornito
  - duration: Singola sessione acuta (2h cycling + 3h recovery): non valuta adattamenti cronici; rilevante per recupero post-gara/allenamento
  - measures: Standard oro: isotopi stabili (L-[ring-2H5]fenilalanina per FSR; [1-13C]leucina per WBNB), biopsie muscolari vastus lateralis a 0 e 4h; glicogeno misurato separatamente
  - transferability: Cicloergometro 2h: durata rappresentativa; intensità non specificata (potenziale confounder); uomini attivi non-elite limitano la generalizzazione a ciclisti competitivi
  - _n=6 è al limite inferiore accettabile: la precisione delle misure con isotopi stabili compensa parzialmente il basso potere statistico_
  - _Finding metodologicamente robusto: PRO-CHO aumenta FSR e rende positivo il WBNB vs entrambi i controlli CHO_
  - _Assenza di effetto di PRO-CHO sulla sintesi glicogena a 1.2 g CHO/kg/h: dato pratico rilevante (non serve aggiungere proteine se il CHO è già ottimale per glicogeno)_
  - _Intensità dell'esercizio non specificata: limite non trascurabile perché intensità modula il breakdown proteico_
  - _Studio necessita replica su campioni più ampi e su atleti allenati/competitivi per incrementare trasferibilità_

## Abstract
Coingestion of protein with carbohydrate (CHO) during recovery from exercise can affect muscle glycogen synthesis, particularly if CHO intake is suboptimal. Another potential benefit of protein feeding is an increased synthesis rate of muscle proteins, as is well documented after resistance exercise. In contrast, the effect of nutrient manipulation on muscle protein kinetics after aerobic exercise remains largely unexplored. We tested the hypothesis that ingesting protein with CHO after a standardized 2-h bout of cycle exercise would increase mixed muscle fractional synthetic rate (FSR) and whole body net protein balance (WBNB) vs. trials matched for total CHO or total energy intake. We also examined whether postexercise glycogen synthesis could be enhanced by adding protein or additional CHO to a feeding protocol that provided 1.2 g CHO·kg−1·h−1, which is the rate generally recommended to maximize this process. Six active men ingested drinks during the first 3 h of recovery that provided either 1.2 g CHO·kg−1·h−1 (L-CHO), 1.2 g CHO + 0.4 g protein·kg−1·h−1 (PRO-CHO), or 1.6 g CHO·kg−1·h−1 (H-CHO) in random order. Based on a primed constant infusion of l-[ ring-2H5]phenylalanine, analysis of biopsies (vastus lateralis) obtained at 0 and 4 h of recovery showed that muscle FSR was higher ( P &lt; 0.05) in PRO-CHO (0.09 ± 0.01%/h) vs. both L-CHO (0.07 ± 0.01%/h) and H-CHO (0.06 ± 0.01%/h). WBNB assessed using [1-13C]leucine was positive only during PRO-CHO, and this was mainly attributable to a reduced rate of protein breakdown. Glycogen synthesis rate was not different between trials. We conclude that ingesting protein with CHO during recovery from aerobic exercise increased muscle FSR and improved WBNB, compared with feeding strategies that provided CHO only and were matched for total CHO or total energy intake. However, adding protein or additional CHO to a feeding strategy that provided 1.2 g CHO·kg−1·h−1 did not further enhance glycogen resynthesis during recovery.
